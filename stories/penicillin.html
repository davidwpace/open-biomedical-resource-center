Chapter 1 of 4Historical evidence, not an experiment you are running
THE MYSTERY · 1/4

Something unusual appeared in a dish.

In 1928, Alexander Fleming noticed mold growing on a plate of bacteria. Around the mold, the bacteria were not growing normally.

Here's the lesson: A surprise is only a clue. The first question is: can we observe it again?

See supporting source ↗

THE CLUE · 2/4

Could the mold be releasing something?

Fleming investigated whether something made by the mold was stopping certain bacteria. He called the substance penicillin.

Here's the lesson: Notice the difference between observing a result and explaining what caused it.

See supporting source ↗

THE TEAMWORK · 3/4

A finding in a dish was not yet a medicine.

Howard Florey, Ernst Chain, Norman Heatley and colleagues helped develop ways to isolate, test and produce penicillin. Moving from laboratory observations to patient treatment required years of additional work.

Here's the lesson: Discovering a promising substance is different from proving it can be used safely and reliably.

See supporting source ↗

THE LESSON · 4/4

Scientific progress often takes many hands.

Penicillin helped transform treatment for many bacterial infections. The original clue mattered, but later testing, production and medical evaluation mattered just as much.

Here's the lesson: One verified observation can be valuable even when someone else performs the next experiment.

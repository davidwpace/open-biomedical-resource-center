Chapter 1 of 5The question
FIRST, THE QUESTION

What if cancer depends on a broken instruction?

Think of a cell as a busy city. Genes help control how messages move around it. When cancer keeps growing, one question is whether a specific message has become stuck in the “on” position.

Your first job: Look for a repeatable difference between cancer cells and ordinary cells. One suspicious gene isn't yet a proven treatment target.

↓ Tap Next: See what they found. I'll guide you.

2002 · THE CLUE

They looked at cancer DNA. One gene kept appearing.

A research team led by Helen Davies examined genes in a known growth-signaling pathway and reported BRAF mutations in 66% of the malignant melanomas they studied. This was a finding in their study samples—not a claim about every melanoma today.

Why this mattered: BRAF became a compelling suspect. But finding a mutation didn't prove that blocking BRAF would help people.

See the original discovery: Davies and colleagues, Nature (2002) ↗

Next, we'll see what that gene does inside a cell.

THE MECHANISM · FOLLOW THE SIGNAL

Imagine the growth message is stuck on.

BRAF helps relay signals through two proteins called MEK and ERK. Some BRAF V600 changes can push the signaling chain into overactivity. This suggested a testable idea: could interrupting the chain slow a susceptible tumor?

2011 · THE CLINICAL TEST

Did a targeted drug actually help people?

In the randomized BRIM-3 trial, 675 people with previously untreated, advanced BRAF V600E melanoma were assigned to receive the BRAF inhibitor vemurafenib or chemotherapy (dacarbazine).

THE SCIENTIFIC THINKING

The surprise isn't just the mutation.

A useful hypothesis has to survive several checks. A gene may be altered in cancer, but that alone doesn't establish that disrupting it will selectively harm cancer cells.

  1. Association: Is BRAF biologically relevant?
  2. Functional evidence: Do cancer models depend on it?
  3. Context: What happens in normal tissues?
  4. Validation: Did clinical studies actually support a treatment in a defined group?

You are examining a proven example of the discovery process—not conducting an experiment on cells.
